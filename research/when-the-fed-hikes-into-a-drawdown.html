When the Fed Hikes Into a Drawdown

First, some personal & company updates:

Hunched forward in my seat, exacerbated by the fact that my 6’3”, 230lb frame doesn’t fit comfortably in the seat in the best conditions, much less so in a completely full bus and the earliest days of large outerwear upon us. Now, to the Fed.

On Friday, November 20, the S&P 500 closed at 6,910.4, down 3.7% on the day and 11.6% under its October 6 closing high of 7,818.9. The VIX jumped 26% to 35.9, the 10-year Treasury yield fell 12bps to 4.92% in the session, and the Fed's target range tops out at 4.00% after a single quarter-point hike in September. The 2-year yield closed at 4.63%, above the policy rate. That combination, a central bank that has only just started raising rates and a stock market that closed Friday more than a tenth below its high, is the kind of setup people reach for a rule about, and the rule people reach for is usually that the Fed will blink.

We wanted to see whether the record supports it. So we went back through every time the S&P 500 closed 10% below a high while policy was in a tightening regime, wrote the rule down before looking at a single outcome, and counted what the Fed did over the next six months. The honest summary is that there is no rule. In 19 earlier episodes the Fed raised rates by a quarter point or more within six months in 9, cut by a quarter point or more in 5, and moved less than a quarter point in 5, with a median move of +0.12 points. A central bank that blinks every time would have produced something very different, and so would one that never does.

Monday took back more than Friday lost. The S&P 500 closed at 7,237.2, up 4.7% on the day and 7.4% under the high, after Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, and the VIX fell to 26.6. Futures moved further toward a December hike, which they now price as close to certain. We date each episode to the first close that meets that test, 10% under the high with policy tightening, so this one is read from Friday's close, the same way the 19 before it are read from theirs, and one day's rally does not take it off the list.

The Rule We Wrote Before We Looked

Any study of this kind can be bent to a conclusion by choosing the episodes, so the episode list comes first and mechanically. An episode is the first close on which the S&P 500 is at least 10% below its running all-time-high close while the policy rate stands at least a quarter point above its lowest level of the previous 18 months. That second condition is a plain way of saying a hiking cycle is under way, or the last hike came recently and cuts have not undone it. We take one episode per drawdown, we start in 1956 because the funds-rate series begins in July 1954 and the rule needs 18 months of it, and for the period before the Fed published a target we use the monthly average of the effective funds rate, which is noisier than a target but good enough to tell a hike from a cut. The rule itself looks only at the prior month's average, because that is what was known on the day; the levels and six-month moves we report use the average of the month in question. The current drawdown goes in as an open episode with no outcome.

Seven drawdowns of 10% or more never satisfied the rule, and we list them rather than hide them: 1950 and 1953, where we have no policy data to test the condition, 1955, where the funds-rate series was only 15 months old and the rule looks back 18, and August 1990 (a separate drawdown from the January 1990 episode we count), 2007, 2020 and 2025, where the tightening condition did not hold. A looser version of the rule, which restarts the count after any 10% rally and so finds many more episodes, gives a messier picture with more cuts in it. We show the strict version because it was the first one written, and we say so because a reader can reasonably want to know.

What the strict version finds is in the second figure. Six months after the first 10% close the policy rate was higher by a quarter point or more in nine episodes, lower by a quarter point or more in five, and had moved less than a quarter point in five. The largest rise came after April 1973 (+2.89 points), the deepest cut after August 1981 (-3.04 points, with the funds rate near 18% and core inflation at 8.9%). The mean move was +0.31 and the median +0.12. If there were a reliable reflex we would see the bars cluster on one side. They do not.

The part that matters for today is how few of the cuts look like a response to the stock market itself. The 1981 cut belongs to a recession the Fed had engineered on purpose. The 1987 cut followed the crash in October of that year, which is the one episode in the sample where the market event was fast and violent enough to be the cause. The 1998 cut came out of the collapse of a large hedge fund and a credit scare. In 1960 and 1990 recessions began within a few months of the -10% close. Strip those away and the equity drop alone, with nothing else going wrong, was usually something the Fed looked through.

Where in the Cycle the Drop Arrives

This drawdown showed up early. At Friday's close the S&P 500 was already 11.6% under its high, 2.1 months after the September 17 hike, which is the effective date of the first increase in this cycle (the decision came a day earlier). We went back through the first hike of each earlier cycle, using the target series, which starts in September 1982, and asked how long it took the index to reach a 10% drawdown. Of the seven earlier cycles that began above that mark (one began already below it), six saw a 10% drawdown within 36 months, at a median of 5.0 months after the first hike. The one that did not was 1994, where the first 10% close came more than three and a half years later, after the Fed's 1994 cycle had ended and a new one had started.

So the speed is not unusual. A stock market that gives back a tenth of its value within a year of the first hike is, if anything, the normal experience of a young cycle, and the normal experience did not stop the Fed in nine of the 19 cases we have. We would not read the timing as a signal in either direction. What it does is remove one argument, which is that a drop this early must mean the cycle is about to be cut short. The record says an early drop has been survivable for the cycle more often than not.

A Real Policy Rate That Is Barely Positive

Here is where today looks different. We define the real policy rate as the top of the target range, or the effective rate before 1982, minus core PCE inflation over the previous twelve months, using the latest month of PCE data that was out at the time (it arrives with a lag of about two months), in today's revised figures. Today that is 4.00% minus 3.12% for September, or +0.88 points. At the 19 earlier -10% closes the median real rate was +3.8 points, and 16 of 19 were positive. Only five of the 19 were lower than today.

The reading cuts two ways, and we think both are fair. On the one hand, a policy rate that is barely above inflation is not restrictive by the standards of most of this sample, which makes it hard to argue that the Fed has already done the damage and is about to see it in the market. The five lower readings are 1956, 2016, 2018 (twice) and 2022; the Fed raised rates in three of those and held in two. On the other hand, a low real rate gives the Fed room to move in either direction without sending a dramatic signal, and the standing view we have held since July, that policy would be late by construction, sits more comfortably with a Fed that finds itself with positive but thin real rates and inflation above 3% than with one that is already tight. We take the evidence as consistent with that view and not as proof of it.

What the Front End Is Pricing

The most informative gap in this record is the one between the 2-year yield and the policy rate, because the 2-year is the market's own forecast of where the funds rate will average over the next two years. When it sits above the policy rate the market expects hikes, and when it sits below it expects cuts. On Friday the 2-year closed at 4.63% against a 4.00% target, a gap of +0.63 points. Among the 12 earlier episodes since 1981 where we have the yield, the median gap was +0.42, and the gap was positive in ten. Friday's reading is tied for the fourth highest of the 13.

Split by what the Fed then did, the gap looks informative. In the episodes that ended in a cut the median gap was -0.05 points, meaning the bond market was already priced flat or for cuts when stocks fell. In the episodes that ended in a hike the median was +0.6. Friday's +0.63 resembles the second group. There is one important exception on the cut side: in October 1987 the 2-year was 1.9 points above the funds rate and the Fed cut anyway, because the crash was fast enough to overrule the pricing. That is the sort of tail the front end does not warn about.

The 10-year tells a different story. It was 5.27% at the October 6 stock high and 5.04% on Thursday, 23bps lower, and it closed Friday at 4.92%, 35bps lower. In 11 of 16 earlier episodes the 10-year yield was higher at the -10% close than at the stock high, and the median change across all 16 was a rise of 24bps. So this time the 10-year has behaved the way it does in a flight to safety, and that is the unusual part. In most of these episodes it rose. We would not make much of it, because a 10-year that falls while the 2-year stays above the funds rate is a combination that can describe an expected-hikes world with a growth scare, and it can describe a lot else.

Jobs Were Not the Separator

If the labor market were the thing that sorted the cuts from the hikes we would see it. We do not. Unemployment had fallen over the prior 12 months at 17 of the 19 earlier -10% closes, with a median change of -0.6 points, rose in one (1990, by 0.1 points) and was flat in another (1968). The cuts of 1987 and 1998 arrived with unemployment falling by 1.1 and 0.4 points. Today the rate is 4.1% for October and is 0.2 points lower than it was a year ago on a September-to-September basis (the October 2025 survey was never collected, so we cannot compute the strict October comparison).

That is a useful negative result. A falling unemployment rate is the usual condition for a stock drop inside a hiking cycle, because hiking cycles are what central banks do when labor markets are tight, and a market drop in that setting has not, by itself, been enough to turn policy. We keep a close watch on the flow data underneath the headline rate, quits and hires and continuing claims, because by the time the unemployment rate moves the story is already over. None of that is in this figure. What the figure says is that the cut episodes did not need a weak labor market first.

Credit Has Crossed the First Line

The high-yield spread closed Friday at 3.76%, 24bps wider on the day, and the Baa-over-10-year-Treasury spread closed at 1.61%, 5bps wider, which puts it in the 13th percentile of its history since 1986. Across the eight earlier -10% closes since 1997 the median high-yield spread was 4.04%, in a range from 2.84% to 7.50%. Friday's reading is tighter than five of the eight.

We want to be careful here, because the comparison with those eight dates turns on a single session: the high-yield spread widened 24bps on Friday alone and the Baa spread 5bps, and at Thursday's 3.52% the high-yield spread was tighter than seven of the eight. The 1998 episode, the closest thing to a credit accident in the cut group, had a high-yield spread of 3.78% on the day the stock market first closed 10% down, which is 2bps from Friday's number. A spread under 4% on that day did not tell us which way the Fed would go: in the four episodes with a reading below 4% (1997, 1998, February 2018 and 2022) the Fed held in one, cut in one and hiked in two. High yield crossed the 350bps line we set on November 2 the day before the drop, so credit has given its first confirmation. At 3.76% it is still tighter than at five of the eight earlier dates, and short of the 4.00% we said on November 12 would show credit pricing the hike.

Volatility Was Louder on Friday Than at Any of the Nine Dates Since 1990

The VIX closed Friday at 35.89, higher than at any of the nine earlier episodes since the index begins in 1990 (the median was 28.2, with a range from 21.5 to 34.3). It was 28.44 a day earlier, so a good part of that is the jump. The more useful way to read it is what followed. Six months after those nine dates the S&P 500 was higher in eight, with a median gain of 10.7% and a range from -12% to +24%. The one decline was 2022, the episode with core inflation at 5.6% and the Fed hiking by almost three points.

Nine observations is a small sample and several of them sit close together in time, so we would not lean on the eight-of-nine, and we would not read it as a prediction about this one. We can say what it shows, which is that in the recent history we can measure, across those nine dates the S&P 500 was higher six months later in eight, and the Fed cut within six months in two. The risk in reading too much into it is that the VIX of 35.9 is a one-day number.

Inflation Is the Hard Part

If one number decides whether the Fed can cut into a stock decline it is inflation. Core PCE was 3.12% for September. At the 18 earlier -10% closes where we have it, the median was 2.0%, and seven of them had inflation at or above today's level. In those seven the Fed hiked in three (1968, 1984, 2022), held in one (1969) and cut in three (1981, 1987, 1990). So even with inflation at this level the cuts have come, but they came with something else attached: a recession the Fed had caused on purpose, a crash, and a downturn that was already visible. With inflation below today's level the Fed hiked six times, held three and cut twice.

The scorecard puts the whole record on one page, with the backdrop at each date and what the Fed then did. Read down the right-hand column and the cuts cluster where several things are true at once. Look at the rows for 1987, 1990 and 1998: the real rate is high (+3.9, +4.5 and +4.5) and core inflation is under 4% in all three. Today's real rate is +0.9. In those cuts the pairing was a restrictive real rate and something the Fed could not ignore: a crash, a downturn, a credit scare. The first half of that pairing is missing today.

What Would Change Our Mind

We are not making a new call here, and the record argues against making one on the stock decline alone. What we have is a read on where the evidence sits, and it sits between the hike group and the cut group, a little closer to the hike group on the front end (a 2-year yield above the funds rate by 0.63 points at Friday's close) and the real rate (+0.9, lower than at any of the five cuts), and split on the inflation (3.1%): at or above that level the Fed hiked in three episodes, held in one and cut in three. We would move toward expecting an easing if the 2-year yield falls through the policy rate, because in three of the four cut episodes where we have the yield it was below the policy rate or within 0.05 points of it; 1987 was the exception. We would move that way if the high-yield spread widens by a point or more in a few sessions, since the closest analog to a credit accident in the sample is 1998. We would also move that way if weekly jobless claims break clearly higher, though the labor record in this issue says it is not required.

We would move the other way, toward expecting more tightening, if core inflation stays at or above 3% in the next two prints and the 2-year stays more than half a point above the policy rate. A VIX that drifts back under 25 within a few weeks without credit moving would also point to a market that took the drop as a repricing and not as a warning. One more thing could change the read: a different episode list. If the strict rule is too narrow, the looser version with more episodes leans toward cuts, and a reader who prefers it will reach a more dovish conclusion than we did. We would rather the disagreement be about the rule than about the arithmetic.

The So-What

For operators, the practical read is that the policy rate you plan around should not be the rate you hope for. In nine of the 19 earlier episodes the Fed had raised rates six months after the stock market's drop, and in 14 of 19 it had not cut. If you borrow on a floating rate or need to refinance in the next year, the front end is currently pricing a funds rate higher than 4%, and the sensible budget keeps that case in view. If you sell into a customer base that is wealthy in stocks, a stock decline of this size has usually arrived with the labor market still tightening (unemployment had fallen over the prior year at 17 of the 19 dates), so it is worth watching order books before cutting plans.

For allocators, the record offers less comfort than the usual story, and it offers some. A Fed put has not been a reliable feature of these episodes: the median six-month policy move was a rise of 0.12 points. At the same time, the median S&P 500 return over the following six months was +6%, and the index was higher in 13 of the 19 (eight of the nine since 1990), which argues against assuming that the next six months are down. Small samples and clustering limit what any of this can prove. The more useful discipline is to decide in advance what you would do if the 2-year yield fell below the funds rate or the high-yield spread jumped a point in a few sessions, because those are the two places we would look first, on a thin record: four cuts with a 2-year yield and one credit accident.
