The Class of 2021

Welcome back to Marginal Matters, the free series where we take one piece of research, sit with it, and walk it through our own data until it makes sense.

This week we're opening the mail from 2021, when the pre-approved card offers were stacked three deep, and following those cards forward to see who is still paying for them.

The Mailbox in 2021

Think back to the mailbox in the spring of 2021. Stimulus checks had landed, a lot of people had spent a year paying down balances because there was nowhere to go, and credit scores all over the country had drifted up as a result. To a card lender running a model, the whole country looked like a better customer than it had in 2019. So the offers went out.

Five economists at the New York Fed, Donghoon Lee, Daniel Mangrum, Joelle Scally, Tejas Sinha and Wilbert van der Klaauw, published a post on Liberty Street Economics yesterday called "A Look at the Wider Divide in Credit Card Delinquency by Income." It rides along with the New York Fed's quarterly report on household debt, and it asks a narrow question that explains a lot: of the people falling behind on their cards today, when did they get the card?

Their answer, boiled down, is that the economy did most of it, and that cards opened during the pandemic, when more of them went to borrowers with lower incomes and lower scores, look like a real second cause. Those cards are now aging out of their worst years, which is why the authors think the surge may prove "more of a pandemic-era echo than a permanent feature of the credit card market."

We took it to our own data. Most of it lands, and the Fed's own bank survey backs the story up. Two things came out differently when we turned the numbers around, and both are worth knowing before you repeat the headline.

Start with the divide itself.

The top panel is the post's first chart, drawn from the data file the authors published with it. In the lowest-income fourth of neighborhoods, 10.3% of card balances went seriously past due over the last year, against 8.0% at the end of 2019. In the highest-income fourth it was 4.4%, against 3.6%. The bottom panel is ours, and we'll get to it in a minute.

What the Number Counts

Two definitions first, because both matter for everything that follows.

The rate in that chart is a flow. Take every dollar of card debt that was in decent standing a quarter ago, meaning less than 90 days behind, and ask how many of those dollars crossed the 90-day line this quarter. Add up four quarters of that and you have the number. The more familiar delinquency rate is a stock, the share of all balances sitting past due on a given day, and it's a bathtub: it shows how much water is in there. The flow is the faucet. It tells you how fast new trouble is arriving, which is why it turns earlier, and why this post uses it.

The second definition is the word income. Credit reports don't carry your paycheck. What the authors know is the zip code, so they sort zip codes by average income from tax returns and cut the population into four equal groups. A retired surgeon in a modest zip code lands in the bottom group, and a line cook renting in a rich one lands in the top. It's a neighborhood divide, which is a blurrier thing than a paycheck divide.

Wider in Points, the Same in Multiples

Now the bottom panel, and the first place we come out somewhere different.

The gap between the bottom and top groups was 4.4 points at the end of 2019. It's 5.9 points now, and it touched 6.3 two years ago. That's the wider divide in the title, and it's real. In dollars of bad debt per hundred, lower-income neighborhoods pulled further away.

Divide instead of subtract, though, and the picture goes flat. The bottom group's rate was 2.24 times the top group's at the end of 2019. Today it's 2.35 times, which is also the average across all of the post's data, back to 2016. Both groups more than doubled off their early 2022 lows, and the top group's rate rose a little more in percentage terms than the bottom's did.

Think of a 30% rent increase landing on a $1,000 apartment and a $3,000 one. It's the same percentage and a very different number of dollars, and the tenant writing the bigger check isn't being singled out. So if you want to know where the bad debt is piling up, read the points, and if you want to know whether something hit lower-income neighborhoods harder than their usual share, read the multiple. Since early 2024 that second answer has been no. The exception came before that, from 2022 through 2023, when the multiple ran as high as 2.8 before settling back. That bulge is where the post's story lives.

How the Door Got Wider

The economy explains why everyone's rate rose. For the bulge, the authors look at who was let in the door during the pandemic, and they show it two ways. The share of new cards going to the lowest-income fourth of neighborhoods went from about 24% before the pandemic to a bit over 26% from the spring of 2020 through the middle of 2022. And the typical credit score on a newly opened card fell from 699 before the pandemic to 688 during it.

Eleven points doesn't sound like much, and the authors flag why it's more than it looks. The drop "likely understates the extent of easing," because everybody's scores were rising at the same time. Lenders let people pause payments without a late mark going on their credit report (forbearance, in the jargon), so the typical score across all borrowers climbed by about a dozen points over the same stretch. It's grade inflation. If the whole class's marks go up twelve points and the students you admit come in eleven points lower, you've reached a lot further down the class than eleven points. Before the pandemic, the typical new cardholder scored 29 points below the typical borrower. For cards opened in 2020 through 2022 that averaged 50, and it reached 62 at the worst.

We lined that up against something the post doesn't use, the Fed's quarterly survey asking banks whether they're tightening or loosening standards on card loans.

It mostly confirms them, with one wrinkle. Banks reported loosening card standards in six straight surveys, from January 2021 through April 2022, and that's when the score at opening slid to its low. The wrinkle is 2020. The share of new cards going to lower-income neighborhoods jumped in the spring of that year, and the score at opening dropped with it, when banks were telling the Fed they were slamming the door, tightening more broadly than in any survey in data back to 1996, the financial crisis included. Our read is that the first jump was about who was still applying for credit in a lockdown, and the true loosening came a year later. The post folds both into one easing period. The survey says there were two different things going on. (One caution on reading it: the survey counts banks, the share tightening minus the share loosening, and says nothing about how far each one moved.)

Every Loan Has a Graduating Class

Here's the idea that makes the post worth your time. Lenders call it a vintage, and it works like a graduating class. Every card belongs to the group it was opened with, and the class carries its admission standards with it for life. The Class of 2021 got in when the door was wide.

The other half is what lenders call seasoning. A new card almost never goes bad in its first months, because it takes time to run up a balance and then time to fall 90 days behind. Trouble builds through the second and third years, peaks, and then fades. The authors put it plainly: newly opened "unseasoned" debts "have historically been the most likely to fall past due." It's a lot like new drivers. The first few years are when the accidents happen, and the ones still on the road after six years are, on average, the careful ones. The post has a nice phrase for that last part. The surviving pandemic cards are a "positively selected" group, since the accounts that were going to blow up mostly already have.

So the overall delinquency rate at any moment is a stack of classes, each at a different point on its own curve.

The orange band is the pandemic class. At its peak in early 2024 it accounted for 3.1 points of the card delinquency rate, more than two-fifths of the total by itself. It's down to 1.8 now, a drop of about 40%. That's the echo fading, just as the post says.

The Next Class Is Already Here

Look at what happened to the total, though. From its peak at the end of 2024, the overall rate has come down only about six-tenths of a point, to 7.1%. The three older groups together gave back 2 full points over that stretch. Cards opened since the start of 2023 added 1.4 of them right back, and that class passed the pandemic one earlier this year to become the biggest slice of the stack.

Some of that is just seasoning, and the post says as much: the newest cards are always the ones most likely to go bad. So a fairer test is to line the classes up by age, which is our second addition.

Three and a half years after each class started opening, the pandemic class was contributing 2.8 points. The class that followed, opened under tighter standards and at higher scores, is at 2.6. The pre-pandemic class was at 2.2, though the relief programs of 2020 bent its curve right at that point.

This is where we part ways with the post a little, and we want to be straight about how much weight it can bear. The clock here starts when each three-year window opens, and the pandemic class got off to a much slower start, so its cards are younger at every mark. The newer class also looks to be leveling off where the pandemic one was still climbing. And these are slices of the overall rate, so a bigger class makes a bigger slice even if every card in it behaves the same, with card balances up from $927 billion at the end of 2019 to $1.29 trillion. We can't see the size of each class in the published data, so we can't say the newer cards are performing like the pandemic ones, card for card. What we can say is that the class with the better scores has replaced most of what the fading class gave up.

And the scores may be flattering it. Cards opened in 2023 through 2025 came with a typical score of 706, above the 699 from before the pandemic. But the typical borrower's score averaged about 743 over those years. Measured against the class instead of the scale, new cardholders sit about 37 points below the middle, compared with 29 before the pandemic. It's the authors' own grade-inflation point, carried forward three years.

How to Read It

A headline delinquency rate is a blend of classes at different ages, so the first question to ask of any move is who's in the stack. A rate can fall because an old class is aging out while a new one is ramping underneath it, and it can rise with nothing wrong except that lenders opened a lot of new accounts two years ago.

Points and multiples answer different questions, the first about where the dollars of bad debt are piling up and the second about whether one group is being hit harder than its usual share, and a headline built on one can look very different built on the other.

And the clocks differ. The New York Fed's report comes about six weeks after each quarter ends. The Fed's bank survey is quarterly too, and it leads, because a loosening shows up in delinquencies two or three years later, once the class has had time to season.

What It's Saying Now

Briefly, since this is the part that changes every quarter.

In the third quarter, card balances rose $22 billion to $1.29 trillion, and the flow into serious delinquency edged down for every income group. The bank-side numbers we track show the same shape with a lag: 2.85% of card loans at commercial banks were at least 30 days past due in the second quarter, down from 3.22% two years earlier, and still above the 2.61% where 2019 ended. Banks are barely tightening anymore, with a net 4% doing so in the October survey. The post, going on the scores of recently opened cards, expects the improvement to continue, "absent macroeconomic shocks."

That last clause carries a lot, and it's where the paycheck comes in. The authors note in passing that inflation has outpaced wage growth for lower-income workers. Here's that in our data.

For about a decade, pay at the bottom grew faster than pay at the top. That flipped in October 2024. As of September, the lowest-paid fourth of workers is getting raises of 3.4% a year while prices are rising 3.7%, and the highest-paid fourth is at 3.8%. A class of cards seasons into its worst years no matter what. Whether it peaks low or high depends a lot on what's happening to the paychecks behind it, and in three of the last six months those have trailed prices at the bottom.

If you run a business that sells to these neighborhoods, or lends into them, the pandemic cards are fading, and the customers to watch over the next year are the ones who opened accounts in 2023 and 2024. They're entering the stretch where a class shows what it is.

In early 2024, cards opened from 2020 through 2022 accounted for 3.1 points of the card delinquency rate, and cards opened since accounted for 0.5. In the third quarter it was 1.8 and 2.7, counting this year's new accounts.
