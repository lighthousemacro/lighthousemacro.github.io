Six Out of Twelve

Welcome back to Marginal Matters, the free series where we take one piece of research, sit with it, and walk it through our own data until it makes sense.

This week we're going back to Econ 101, to the chapter on how the Fed sets interest rates, because two people at the Fed graded that chapter a few years ago and the best mark in the class was a 50.

The Chapter You Probably Remember

If you took intro economics at any point in the last half century, you learned some version of this. The Fed wants lower rates, so it buys bonds. The money lands in banks as reserves, the banks lend most of it out, those loans come back as deposits and get lent again, and by the time that multiplier has run its course there's more money around and it costs less to borrow. There were three tools on the exam: buying and selling bonds (open market operations, in the jargon), the reserve requirement, and the discount rate.

Jane Ihrig, then an economist at the Federal Reserve Board, and Scott Wolla, then the economic education coordinator at the St. Louis Fed, published a paper in October 2020 called "Let's Close the Gap." They took six big-name principles textbooks, all 2020 or 2021 editions, and scored the monetary policy chapter in each one on 15 concepts. A book earned a point for every piece of the Fed's current system it got right and lost a point for every holdover from the old one. A perfect score was +12, and the worst possible was -3.

A chart of the results has been going around X this week, six years after the fact, and it sent us back to read the whole paper. Their finding, boiled down, is that the Fed changed how it sets interest rates after 2008, said in January 2019 that the change was here to stay, and most of the books kept the old chapter and stapled the new tools onto the back of it.

We took it to our own data, where the mechanism they describe shows up about as cleanly as anything does in this business. We also checked what has happened since, and the ending has a twist the authors had no way to see coming.

Start with the grades.

Half the books came in below zero, Mankiw's among them. The best of the six, by Stevenson and Wolfers, got to 6 on a scale that tops out at 12.

It matters more than a grading exercise usually does. The paper cites estimates that about 40% of undergraduates take an introductory economics course, roughly two million students a year, and that most of them never take another.

When Reserves Were Scarce

The old chapter was right when it was written. It described a real system, and that system ran on scarcity.

Reserves are the balances banks keep in their accounts at the Fed, the money banks use to pay each other. Before 2008 there weren't many. In the Fed's weekly balance sheet data, banks held about $11 billion on average from 2003 through 2007 (the paper puts the pre-crisis level at $20 billion), in a banking system with about $8.6 trillion in assets. A bank that came up short at the close borrowed overnight from one that had extra, and the rate on that loan is the fed funds rate.

When something is that scarce, its price jumps with every small change in supply, and the old system put that to work. Each day the trading desk at the New York Fed estimated how many reserves the banks needed and bought or sold securities to add a little or drain a little. Think of a lot downtown with forty parking spaces on a Friday night. Take five away and people start circling the block.

Then came 2008.

The Fed bought assets by the trillion during the crisis, and every purchase created reserves. By October 2014 banks held $2.79 trillion of them. The pandemic took the total to a peak of $4.28 trillion in December 2021, and it stands at $2.96 trillion today, about 277 times the pre-2008 average.

You can't steer a price by adding five spaces to a lot that has grown from forty to eleven thousand. From last December until this September, a stretch when the Fed's target didn't change, reserves swung by an average of $48 billion a week, more than four times the entire stock banks held before 2008, and in one week they fell by $228 billion. The fed funds rate printed 3.62%, 3.63% or 3.64% on every one of those 193 trading days.

So the old lever had come unhooked, and something else was doing the work.

A Price on a Sign

Here's the something else, and the authors make a point we'd underline: the new system is easier to explain than the old one.

Since October 2008 the Fed has paid banks interest on their reserves. Today that rate is 3.90%. If you're a bank with spare cash, you can leave it at the Fed overnight and collect 3.90% with no risk and no effort, so you aren't going to lend it to another bank for less. The paper calls this a reservation rate, "the lowest rate that counterparties are willing to accept for lending out their funds." It's the same reason you wouldn't lend your brother-in-law money at 3% when your savings account pays 4. And if the market rate slipped well under the Fed's, a bank could borrow in the market, park the money at the Fed and keep the difference, which pulls the market rate back up.

Not everybody with cash can earn that rate, though. A handful of government-sponsored lenders that keep accounts at the Fed can't, and they lend in the fed funds market, which is why the market rate prints a hair under the Fed's rate. Money market funds can't either. So the Fed gives them their own window (the overnight reverse repurchase agreement facility, if you want the full name) at a slightly lower rate, 3.75% today, and that works as a floor. Above it all sits the discount rate, what the Fed charges a bank that borrows from it directly, 4.00% today. It's meant to be the ceiling, though banks hate being seen using it, and the authors say plainly that the stigma weakens it. (A newer backstop the paper predates, the standing repo facility, sits at the same 4.00%.)

So the Fed posts three prices on a sign and the market rate settles in among them. When it wants to raise rates, it changes the sign.

We can watch it happen. On September 16 the Fed raised its target range a quarter point, to 3.75% to 4.00%. The same afternoon it published a short implementation note moving all three posted rates up a quarter point, effective the next morning. The fed funds rate was 3.63% on the 16th and 3.88% on the 17th.

That's one move. Since the Fed first raised rates this way in December 2015, the rate on reserves has changed 35 times, and on 32 of them the fed funds rate moved by the same amount, give or take a basis point (a hundredth of a percentage point), on the same day. The three exceptions were that very first hike, when fed funds rose 22 of the 25, the week the repo market seized up in September 2019, and the second emergency cut of March 2020.

Our favorite piece of evidence is a smaller one. Twice in 2018 the Fed raised its target range by 25 basis points and the rate on reserves by only 20. The fed funds rate rose 20 both times. Offered a choice between the announcement and the sign, the market went with the sign.

Over those 2,741 trading days the fed funds rate printed outside the Fed's range twice: on New Year's Eve 2015, by 5 basis points on the low side, and on September 17, 2019, by 5 on the high side. Hold on to that second date for a minute.

The Picture Missing From All Six

Of the paper's 15 concepts, three are about a diagram, and they tell you the most. Did the book draw the new system anywhere? None of the six did. Four of them still drew the old one.

The rest of the scorecard reads the same way. Four of the six still told students that buying and selling bonds is the Fed's main tool; Mankiw's text, as the paper quotes it, says "open market operations are the tool of monetary policy that the Fed uses most often." Only three got credit for naming interest on reserves as the primary tool, and not one explained what the bond-buying is for these days, which is keeping reserves plentiful enough that the sign keeps working.

The picture they're missing is a demand curve with a bend in it. When reserves are scarce, banks will pay up for them, and the curve is steep, so a little less supply means a much higher rate. Past some point banks have all they need, an extra dollar of reserves is worth only what the Fed pays on it, and the curve goes flat at that rate. The old system lived on the steep part and the new one sits on the flat part on purpose, which is the paper's whole definition of the word: "To be 'ample' the supply curve must intersect the demand curve where it is flat."

It's the parking lot again. When the lot is half empty, the price of a space is whatever's posted at the gate. Get down to the last twenty spaces and people start cutting deals in the aisles.

So we drew it ourselves, with real weeks in place of a sketch.

Each faint dot is a week, and the blue line is the average for weeks with that level of reserves. Start on the right, where reserves were plentiful. In the 433 weeks when they were 12% of bank assets or more, the fed funds rate never once averaged above the Fed's rate, and ran about 7 basis points under it. That's the flat part.

Now the left side. Reserves fell below 9% of bank assets for 31 weeks, every one of them between April and December of 2019, and the fed funds rate was above the Fed's rate in 28 of those. The orange dot at the top left is the week of September 18, 2019, with reserves at 8.0% of bank assets and fed funds 11 basis points over. That week includes the date we asked you to hold on to. The one time in eleven years that the rate broke out the top of the Fed's range was the week the system had slid farthest up the steep part of a curve the textbooks hadn't drawn.

One caution before anybody frames this. You'll notice the dots don't fall on one tidy line, and we wouldn't expect them to, because the gap between the floor and the rate on reserves has been reset a few times (25 basis points in 2016, 10 in 2023 and 15 now) and because the amount of reserves banks want isn't a fixed number. You can't observe the curve directly, and neither can the Fed, which has to infer it from dots like these.

What Falls Out of the Old Chapter

Two things the old chapter leaned on are gone, and both show up in our data.

The first is the reserve requirement. Required reserves were $208 billion in February 2020, and the Fed cut the requirement to zero that March.

The second is the money multiplier, the part of the story where a dollar of new reserves turns into several dollars of money as banks lend it out and it comes back around.

Divide the money supply (M2) by the monetary base (currency plus reserves) and you get the multiplier as it worked out in practice. In August 2008 it was 9.2. By August 2014 it was 2.8, and it's 4.3 today. The base is 6.4 times what it was in August 2008, and M2 is 3.0 times.

The old chapter treats reserves like a hot potato that every bank is trying to lend out as fast as it can, with the money supply multiplying along the way. What happened instead is that the Fed created trillions in reserves, the money supply didn't multiply to match, and paying interest on those reserves is what lets the Fed set the price of money anyway. That's why the authors docked the books that described interest on reserves as a way to manage the quantity of money. They grant that the link is valid, but it sends students off looking at how much money there is, when the Fed is working on what it costs. Their proposed rewrite of the AP Macroeconomics guidelines dropped the multiplier bullet altogether.

Why did the chapter survive so long? The paper cites a rule of thumb from textbook publishing that a principles text can't differ from the standard one by more than about 15%, because professors won't rewrite their lecture notes for it. A second pull, a sales test for revised editions, nudges each one closer to the common standard. The book that scored highest was the only first edition in the group. In the authors' words it was "relieved of the baggage of previous editions."

How to Read It

When the Fed changes rates it puts out two documents at the same time. The statement gets the headlines. The implementation note does the work, because that's where the posted rates are, and the one the market hugs is the rate on reserves: fed funds has been between 1 and 3 basis points below it on every trading day this year.

When you read that the Fed is buying Treasury bills, check what for. In this system those purchases are maintenance, the thing that keeps the lot from filling up, and that's different from buying bonds to push long-term rates down even though the transaction looks the same from the sidewalk.

What It's Saying Now

Briefly, since this is the part that changes.

Some of the class has caught up in six years. The College Board's current AP Macroeconomics framework says the U.S. banking system has ample reserves and that "the Federal Reserve's key policy tool is interest on reserves," close to word for word what Ihrig and Wolla proposed. We haven't re-scored the newest editions of the six books, and we won't guess at their grades.

Today's sign reads 3.90% on reserves, 3.75% at the floor and 4.00% at the discount window, and the fed funds rate was 3.88% on November 17. Reserves are $2.96 trillion, or 11.3% of bank assets, which puts the dark dot on that chart at the bend in the curve. Last December the Fed said reserves had "declined to ample levels" and started buying Treasury bills to hold them there, and its balance sheet has grown by about $220 billion since. That's one of the concepts none of the six textbooks covered, and it's happening in real time.

Here's the twist. In June, at his first press conference as chair, Kevin Warsh announced a task force on the balance sheet that will, in his words, "review the benefits and risks of the current ample-reserves regime" and "assess alternative frameworks." He said he hoped most of his task forces would wrap up by year-end. The September and October Fed statements both say the Committee "is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves." We haven't seen findings yet and we don't know where it lands. It would be a very economics outcome, though, if the textbooks finished catching up to the system in the same year the Fed reopened the question.

If you run a business, the practical version is short. When the Fed moves, the rate on a floating loan moves with it, because the benchmark many of those loans reset against lives among the same posted prices (SOFR went from 3.62% to 3.85% the day the September hike took effect). The sign changes and the price changes, with no wave of money working its way through the banks first.

Since December 2015, the rate the Fed pays on reserves has changed 35 times, and the fed funds rate went with it, to within a basis point, on 32.

Four of the six textbooks said the tool was something else.
